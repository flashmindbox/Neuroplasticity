# L3 · Critical & multi-angle thinking

[← Handbook](../README.md)

**Dimensions served:** 8 Critical & multi-angle thinking, 9 Judgement, 13 Integrity & empathy.
**Evidence grade:** B (explicit critical-thinking instruction + practice; learning by teaching) · A (specificity of practice — train on exam-format material).

## Why it matters for UPSC
Mains rewards answers that see an issue from several sides, connect causes and consequences, and reach a
balanced conclusion with a practical way forward. The board tests "critical powers of assimilation" and
"balance of judgement". These are thinking *habits* — and habits are trainable.

## How it works
- Critical thinking improves with **explicit instruction in thinking moves plus practice**, not by exposure
  alone (Abrami et al., 2015).
- **Frameworks reduce cognitive load** — when the lenses are automatic, working memory is free for content
  (Sweller, 1988). This matters even more under exam time pressure.
- **Specificity** — the drills use the kinds of issues UPSC asks about, so the habit transfers.

## The thinking moves

### Move 1 — The 7 lenses (multi-angle)
For any issue, run through: **Social · Economic · Political · Legal/Constitutional · Ethical · Environmental ·
Administrative** (add *International* or *Technological* when relevant). Aim for at least one specific point per lens.

### Move 2 — Steelman
State the *strongest* version of the view you disagree with — so well its supporters would agree. Then respond.
This is the core of "balance of judgement".

### Move 3 — Causal chain
"X → leads to Y → which causes Z…" at least three links deep, plus one feedback loop. Ask "and then what?"

### Move 4 — Stakeholder map
Who gains, who loses, who decides, who is invisible? (Women, children, tribal communities, future generations,
the environment.)

### Move 5 — Think like a DM
"It's Monday morning in your district and *[problem]* lands on your desk." Immediate steps (24 h) → short-term
(1 month) → systemic fix (1 year). Who do you call? What's the legal basis? What can go wrong?

### Move 6 — First principles
What is the problem *really*? Strip away the jargon. What would have to be true for a solution to work?

### Move 7 — Pre-mortem
"It's two years later and this policy failed. Why?" (Klein, 2007)

## Exercises & dose
- **Daily drill (15 min):** one prompt from the [thinking bank](../drills/thinking-prompts.md), one move, timed.
  Score yourself on the rubric first; then optionally request the AI evaluation.
- **Weekly teaching (20 min):** explain one issue to your accountability partner or record it as if teaching —
  expecting to teach improves organisation of knowledge (Nestojko et al., 2014).
- **Weekly dilemma (20 min):** one [ethical dilemma](../drills/dilemmas.md) with stakeholders, options and principles.

## Drill rubric (scored 1–5 each)
1. **Breadth** — relevant angles covered
2. **Depth** — specific, concrete points (not generic labels)
3. **Balance** — opposing views fairly represented
4. **Causality** — mechanisms, not just lists
5. **Practicality** — a realistic way forward

## Progression
- **L1** 3–4 lenses in 3 minutes, mostly generic.
- **L2** 5–6 lenses in 90 seconds; a credible steelman.
- **L3** 7+ specific angles, causal chains and stakeholders without prompting.
- **L4** You find the non-obvious angle and the central trade-off, fast — in writing and in speech.

## Objective markers
Angles per 90 s · rubric scores (self vs AI) · drill time · steelman quality.

## Common traps
- Listing lens names without specific content ("economic angle: important").
- Always concluding "both sides have merit" — balance is not fence-sitting; take a reasoned position.
- Drills on topics unrelated to the syllabus.
